How it works

An overview of how calls are made, how they are graded, and what the scorecard can and cannot tell you. This is a hobby project that collects public data and makes reports. It is not investment advice.

What is predicted

For a fixed list of US and European stocks, ETFs and currencies, over three horizons:

  • Today's session: from today's open to today's close.
  • Next week: 5 trading days.
  • Next 3 months: 63 trading days.
Call Meaning
Bullish expected to do better than its benchmark (for today's session: close above the open)
Neutral expected to stay within a normal range
Bearish expected to do worse than its benchmark (for today's session: close below the open)

Benchmarks: the S&P 500 (SPY) for US names and the STOXX Europe 600 for European names. Currencies and indices are graded on their own price move.

How a call is made

Public data. For each instrument, the desk gathers prices, recent news from about 20 sources, prediction-market odds, company data and a macro picture.

A decision board. Several AI members look at that data, each from a different angle, and each votes Bullish, Neutral or Bearish with its reasons. Members can see a summary of the others' views and change their minds before the final vote.

The call is computed. No single model chooses the board's call. It is worked out from the members' weighted votes. The council pages show the vote count and how clearly the call won.

The board learns. As calls are graded, members that read the market well count for more and members that read it badly count for less.

Integrity

  • Recorded before the outcome. Every call is stored with a timestamp in a hash chain when it is made. Each record's SHA-256 fingerprint covers its content and the previous record's fingerprint, so past calls cannot be edited quietly.
  • Nothing is deleted. A call that must not count, such as a test run, is excluded with a published reason.
  • Losses are shown. The scorecard lists every graded call, right or wrong.

How a call is graded

  • Entry and exit. The entry is the last known price when the call was made. The exit is the close at the end of the horizon.
  • Excess move. The instrument's return minus its benchmark's return over the same days.
  • Normal range. ±0.5 × the instrument's usual volatility over that horizon. A move inside it counts as flat.
  • Right or wrong. Bullish is right if the instrument beat the range, Bearish if it fell below it, Neutral if it stayed inside.
  • Baseline. The scorecard compares the board with simply calling everything Bullish. A board that can't beat that adds nothing.

Session calls

  • When. Before each market opens: the European names before 09:00 Frankfurt time, the US names before 09:30 New York time. The calls appear in that morning's brief.
  • What. Whether each instrument closes above or below today's opening price. A move under half a typical day counts as flat, which is what a Neutral call predicts.
  • Why the open. The call is recorded before the open, so the opening price is the first price nobody could have known. A call recorded after the open is kept but never graded.
  • Scored. After the US close, the Day in review edition shows each morning call against the close, the day's tally next to always-Bullish, and the last 30 days' record.

What the scorecard can't tell you

One day, or a handful of calls, is noise. Look at the 30-day record and its range, not a single session. Past accuracy says nothing certain about future calls. Prices come from free data sources and can be delayed or occasionally wrong.

Proof the record hasn't been edited

Every call and brief is stored in a chain where each entry's fingerprint (a SHA-256 hash) covers its content and the entry before it. Changing anything in the past would change the latest fingerprint below. Save today's value, and you can check later that the history still leads to it.
